# Brief voice

A brief is read under time pressure by someone whose day is measured in
minutes. Every rule here serves one test: can the speaker find and absorb a
line in the car to the venue.

This skill is deliberately organisation-free — no names, no figures, no
domain terms — so it can be lifted into another writing surface unchanged,
the way CPLAN's `chart-standards` is.

## Language

- Every brief is written in English, whatever the engagement's language —
  house rule, not a per-engagement choice. A quote from a source stays
  verbatim in its original language (the verbatim rule below), followed by
  a one-line English gloss; everything else, including facts drawn from
  German-language sources, is written in English.
- Address the speaker directly as "you" ("You will be introduced by…").
  The brief is for one reader; third-person distance wastes their time.
- Active voice, present tense for the event's facts, no nominalisation
  chains. "The moderator opens with X" — not "the opening of the session
  will be undertaken by".

## Sentence and length budgets

- Sentences: one clause preferred, two maximum. A sentence needing a third
  clause becomes two sentences.
- Paragraphs: four lines maximum on the printed page.
- Section budgets: at-a-glance one page; objective one paragraph; each key
  message three lines; each Q&A entry five lines; each biography three
  sentences. A section over budget is cut, not squeezed into smaller type.
- The whole brief: aim for six printed pages, never more than ten
  including appendices.

## Form

- Sentence case everywhere — headings included. No ALL CAPS, no
  underlining, consistent with the design system's constraints.
- Bold marks the line the speaker must not miss — at most one bolded line
  per section. Bold everywhere is bold nowhere.
- Facts the speaker will look up mid-event (times, names, room, phone
  numbers) are set on their own line, never buried mid-paragraph.
- Lists for parallel facts; prose for anything with a because. A bullet
  list of reasoning reads as facts and hides the argument.

## Words that never appear

- Superlatives and leadership claims ("best", "unique", "world-class").
- Hedges that transfer risk to the speaker ("arguably", "one could say").
- Filler adjectives ("very", "extremely", "incredibly").
- Jargon the audience section does not justify. An internal abbreviation
  appears once with its expansion, then may be used.

## What voice never overrides

Voice rules bend to accuracy, never the reverse. A quote from a source is
reproduced verbatim even when it breaks every budget above; a fact that
needs an awkward sentence to be true gets the awkward sentence. When a
rewrite for tone would change what a line claims, the rewrite is wrong.
