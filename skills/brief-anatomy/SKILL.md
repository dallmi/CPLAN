# Speaker brief anatomy

A brief is the same document every time. The speaker reads it in the car to
the venue; a section that moves around between briefs costs them the thirty
seconds they actually have. The order below is fixed. A section with nothing
to say is printed with the line "Nothing to report" — an absent section reads
as a forgotten one, and the speaker cannot tell the difference.

> **Draft status:** this anatomy is a pre-Phase-0 hypothesis. Correct it
> against real briefs before building on it.

## Sections, in order

1. **Engagement at a glance** — one page maximum. Event, date and time,
   venue, format and duration, host, audience (who and how many), other
   speakers, the one-line objective. Everything here is a fact from the
   engagement record, never generated.
2. **Objective and desired outcome** — one paragraph. What should be true
   after the appearance that was not true before. If nobody can say, that is
   a finding to surface to the owner, not a gap to write around.
3. **Key messages** — exactly three. Rules in the `key-messages` skill.
4. **Audience profile** — who is in the room, what they already believe,
   what they came for, known sensitivities. Grounded in the engagement
   record and the context pack, not invented.
5. **Context and environment** — what the organisation has said recently to
   this audience or on this topic, other executive appearances in the same
   window, collisions. Built exclusively from the context pack; rules in the
   `engagement-context` skill.
6. **Flow** — the run of the event from the speaker's point of view: arrival,
   entrance, speaking slot, handovers, exit. Facts only.
7. **Anticipated Q&A** — rules in the `qa-anticipation` skill.
8. **Biographies** — moderator, co-panelists, host. Three sentences each,
   relevance to the speaker first ("you last met at…" when known), career
   summary second.
9. **Logistics and contacts** — venue access, on-site contact with phone
   number, tech setup, dress code. Facts only.
10. **Sources** — every document the generated sections drew on, by name and
    date. A brief that cannot name its sources cannot be reviewed.

## What varies by format

The set above is the keynote baseline. Formats add — they never reorder or
remove:

| Format | Adds |
|---|---|
| Panel | Co-panelist positioning in §8: each panelist's likely line, where the speaker agrees and where not |
| Fireside / interview | Interviewer's style and recent interviews in §8; likely arc of the conversation in §6 |
| Town hall (internal) | Current employee sentiment and open internal questions in §4 |
| Media | Red lines in §7: questions to decline and refer — see the referral rule in `qa-anticipation` |

## Generated versus factual

Sections 1, 6, 9 and the facts in 8 are **assembled** from records and must
not be paraphrased, embellished, or filled by guessing — a wrong room number
is worse than an empty one. Sections 2, 3, 4, 7 and the positioning half of 8
are **drafted** and every one of them carries the marker line
`Draft — not yet reviewed` until a named reviewer removes it. Section 5 is
assembled from the context pack and reads like prose but invents nothing.

## Completeness

A brief is complete when every section is present, every generated section
has lost its draft marker, and §10 lists at least one source for each drafted
section. "Complete" is a checkable property, not an impression — a reviewer
asked "is this brief done" answers by walking this list.
